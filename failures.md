# When Tools Fail

Every razor, bias, and model has boundary conditions where it misleads rather than helps. Knowing *when not to apply* a tool is as important as knowing when to apply it.

---

## Razors

### Occam's Razor Failed
**Context:** 2008 Financial Crisis
**Why it failed:** Multiple interacting causes (leverage, securitization, ratings fraud, regulatory capture, moral hazard), not one simple explanation.
**Better tool:** System Dynamics (3.21) + Feedback Loops (3.12). Hickam's Dictum (1.6).

### Hanlon's Razor Failed
**Context:** Enron and corporate fraud scandals
**Why it failed:** Attributing systemic fraud to "incompetence" delayed investigation. The behavior *was* malice, deliberately concealed as complexity.
**Better tool:** Incentives Razor (1.10) — when incentives strongly reward deception, assume it until proven otherwise.

### Chesterton's Fence Failed
**Context:** Legacy regulations blocking innovation (e.g., taxi medallion systems vs. ride-sharing)
**Why it failed:** The "fence" existed to protect incumbents, not serve public interest. Understanding the original purpose doesn't mean the purpose is still valid.
**Better tool:** First Principles (3.1) — rebuild from current needs, not historical justifications.

## Biases (Overcorrection)

### Overcorrecting for Confirmation Bias
**Context:** Intelligence agencies post-9/11
**Why it failed:** So focused on avoiding confirmation bias that analysts second-guessed clear signals, creating paralysis and information overload.
**Better tool:** Expected Value (3.4) — weigh cost of false negatives vs. false positives rather than seeking perfect neutrality.

### Overcorrecting for Sunk Cost
**Context:** Premature project abandonment
**Why it failed:** Teams kill projects at the first sign of difficulty, citing "sunk cost fallacy," when persistence through a hard phase would have yielded returns.
**Better tool:** Optionality (3.5) — ask whether the project still has option value, not just whether past costs are recoverable.

## Mental Models

### First Principles Failed
**Context:** Theranos
**Why it failed:** "First principles thinking" was used to dismiss domain expertise. Physics-level reasoning can't substitute for biological and engineering constraints without deep domain knowledge.
**Better tool:** Margin of Safety (3.6) — respect known constraints; verify before dismissing expert consensus.

### Expected Value Failed
**Context:** Personal decisions with irreversible consequences (health, safety)
**Why it failed:** EV is designed for repeatable bets. A 1% chance of death is not equivalent to a 1% loss on 100 trades. You only die once.
**Better tool:** Margin of Safety (3.6) + Barbell Strategy (3.19) — for non-repeatable, catastrophic risks, avoid ruin first.

### Mean Reversion Failed
**Context:** Regime changes (e.g., post-gold-standard inflation, digital disruption of media)
**Why it failed:** Mean reversion assumes a stable system. When the underlying structure changes, there is no "mean" to revert to.
**Better tool:** Second-Order Effects (3.2) — ask whether the system itself has changed, not just the data.

## Game Theory

### Prisoner's Dilemma (Tit-for-Tat) Failed
**Context:** Middle East peace negotiations
**Why it failed:** Tit-for-tat escalates when noisy signals cause misattribution. Each side "retaliates" against perceived defection that was actually noise.
**Better tool:** Generous tit-for-tat (occasionally cooperate after defection) + Principle of Charity (1.8).

### Nash Equilibrium Failed
**Context:** Financial markets during panics
**Why it failed:** Nash equilibrium assumes common knowledge and rationality. In crises, bounded rationality, information cascades, and panic dominate.
**Better tool:** Feedback Loops (3.12) + System Dynamics (3.21) — model the cascade, not the equilibrium.

---

## Meta-Lesson

No single tool is universally correct. The toolkit's value is in **triangulation** — applying multiple frameworks and checking where they agree and disagree. When tools conflict, that's a signal to investigate further, not to pick the most convenient answer.
